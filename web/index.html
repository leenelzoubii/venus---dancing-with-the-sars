What am I looking at?

This is radar, not a photograph. NISAR sends a pulse down and measures how much of it comes back. Smooth open water bounces the pulse away from the satellite, so it appears dark. Rough surfaces — shoreline salt crusts, vegetation, built ground — scatter the pulse back and appear bright.

  • Date shows one acquisition.
  • Swipe puts the earliest date on the left and the chosen date on the right.
  • Change subtracts the two in decibels. Blue means less radar echo came back on the later date — smoother or wetter, as with new water. Red means more echo — rougher or drier.
  • HH and HV are two ways of listening. HV is quieter and more sensitive to vegetation and water; HH is louder and responds to surface roughness and structure.

Because radar sees through cloud and darkness, this works at night and in haze — the same reason we can map the surface of Venus, a planet wrapped in permanent cloud.

Limitations and honesty notes
  • One geometry only. Every date is descending track 151, frame 073. Mixing passes would fake change that is not there.
  • Provisional data. These products are calibrated but not fully validated; reprocessing is expected. Numbers may shift slightly.
  • Water masks are thresholded. Wind roughens water and makes it brighter; wet salt crust can be bright too. Each area is quoted with a band, not a single number.
  • Scene offset removed. A whole-scene brightness offset between dates is corrected before differencing, and the value is stated in the legend.
  • Change below our uncertainty is called that. A small difference is not a trend.
  • Instrument gap. There is a planned NISAR data gap between 2026-07-27 and 2026-08-10, so the series is not evenly spaced there.
